The Project & The Author

About This Project

The Post-Break Purge & Tech Turnover began as a simple question: where does all my stuff go? As a Computer Science student at Rutgers University, I spend a significant portion of my budget on technology—laptops, smartwatches, cables, peripherals—and yet I rarely pause to think about what happens to these objects once I'm done with them. This project is an attempt to change that by documenting one week's worth of disposals in honest, sometimes uncomfortable detail.

The idea was partly inspired by the work of archaeologist Sarah Newman, who has argued that studying what a society throws away reveals as much about its values as studying what it keeps. Newman's research into ancient middens (trash heaps) shows that disposal is never a neutral act—it reflects priorities, anxieties, and blind spots. For a college student cycling through tech upgrades every few semesters, the parallel felt uncomfortably apt.

I was also struck by the art of Vik Muniz, the Brazilian artist who creates stunning portraits out of scrap metal, bottle caps, and discarded electronics. Muniz's work forces viewers to confront the sheer volume of material we discard daily—and to see beauty in what's been abandoned. While I'm not making gallery art out of my old USB cables, the spirit is similar: paying attention to the stuff we usually ignore.

On a more sobering note, the World Health Organization estimates that global e-waste generation reached 62 million tonnes in 2022 and is projected to hit 82 million tonnes by 2030. Only about 22% of that is formally collected and recycled. The rest ends up in landfills or is informally processed under hazardous conditions in developing countries. Even a single student's weekly output—a laptop here, a watch there—is part of that global flow.

This project doesn't pretend to solve any of those problems. But by tracking nine items over one week—recording their weight, cost, condition, and method of disposal—it aims to make the invisible visible. The hope is that this kind of small-scale self-monitoring can shift habits: buying more intentionally, maintaining things longer, and choosing recycling or resale over the trash can whenever possible. Think of it as a Fitbit for your garbage.

About the Author

Brian Z.

Brian is a Computer Science student at Rutgers University—New Brunswick, currently living in the Busch Campus apartments. He spends most of his time debugging code, researching the latest tech specs, and occasionally remembering to eat something that isn't from King of Gyro.

When he's not studying, Brian works part-time in retail and tinkers with personal projects ranging from web development to game design. He has a particular interest in how people interact with technology—not just when they buy it, but when they decide to let it go. This project grew out of that curiosity, plus a very real need to reclaim desk space before midterms.
